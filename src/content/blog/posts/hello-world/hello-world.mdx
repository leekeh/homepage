Today is the day I'm launching my new personal site. It's been a while in the making, and to celebrate it I wanted to sit down and write some of the thoughts behind it.

## Craving the old internet

It's been a little over 5 years since I started working as a web developer. Besides brief encounters [customizing tumblr themes](https://help.tumblr.com/knowledge-base/customizing-your-theme/), I mainly learned to code as a profession. That means working on tasks with tightly defined designs, aligned to a marketable design language. Nothing wrong with that inherently, companies need a recognizable style that is easy on the eyes of their customers. But I've also been thinking a bit more about the history of the internet. More and more, the web seems to be a corporate place, a place where people interact with businesses. Either to purchase something, manage some tasks, do work, or to just get sucked into an algorithm. And that's not even mentioning the [dead internet theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory), where content on social media is generated by bots, and the people you interact with are not real. AI seems to be a major accellerator of this pre-existing trend.

That's not how the internet started. Originally, it was a decentralized network where anyone could create their own niche. People used the limited capabilities of the web to play and experiment, share their ideas, without any ulterior motives (like making money). Honestly speaking, I was not really there to experience the early web. By the time I started using the internet, it was the era of early social media, think [myspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myspace) (or in my case [hyves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyves)). While they were still social media, there was way more opportunity for custom styles, adding widgets, playing your favorite music, etc. Nowadays it all seems a bit more standardized and focused on appealing to the widest audience possible.

This observation is really not a very unique one. A lot of people seem unhappy about the current state and experience of the internet. People are tired of being constantly marketed towards. Many are simply [logging off](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/07/young-people-quiet-revolution-social-media.html). But while big businesses demand a lot of bandwidth, it does not mean that that is the only way to make websites. The underlying principles of the web have not changed. You can still write some HTML, put it up on a server and create your own custom corner of the internet. And people still do.

Lately, I've been into browsing [neocities](https://neocities.org/). It's a platform where people can upload their own custom website and host it for free. Browsing around feels like a breath of fresh air. Designs are different, flashy, awkward, or even ugly. They often don't work on different browsers or screen sizes. It's mainly a group of people exploring, showcasing their art, writing their blogs, filling in each others guestbooks. They are making it up, learning how code works, along the way. It's a taste of what it was like back in the day. And it's alive.

### Retro OS

A specific aesthetic that pops up on neocities (and elsewhere) is websites that are made to look like older operating systems. This ranges from very early versions of MacOS to Windows XP and Vista. Nostalgia often plays a big part in this aesthetic. The visual language is familiar, yet it opens the way for experimentation. You can hide a lot of fun stuff on these pages, all kinds of widgets and pages only discovered by exploring. It fit exactly what I was looking for, something different and a place where I can park different bits of content.

Still, pages aligned with this style can often have their limitations. I wanted to tackle them one by one, to highlight the values I have as a developer. These challenges are responsive design, accessibility, and progressive enhancement. It is difficult to marry these with such an experimental style, and it certainly requires some extra effort. So let me quickly highlight how I did it.

### Responsive design

The Retro OS aesthetic is based on a desktop design. Inherently, this makes it difficult to make it nice or even usable on mobile. In my explorations, I found that people would either use the same layout on desktop and mobile, which makes it tricky to organize open windows and navigate between views, or they would just outright disable the website on mobile.

After thinking about it for a while, I decided to have a separate design for smaller screens. Instead of focusing on a desktop operating system with a task bar and draggable windows, I went for a different, kind of vintage, UX pattern: tabs. They are still used from time to time to navigate between different different views of a content section or graph, but you don't really see them as much anymore for top-level navigation.

### Progressive enhancement

It's debatable whether a website should work without JavaScript. On the one hand, JavaScript is an important factor in probably most of the websites you visit. Frontend frameworks, most prominently [React](https://react.dev/), allow developers to create highly dynamic and interactive websites. Especially in a project like mine, there is a lot of complexity in managing windows. Users can minimize windows, drag and resize them, and this state is restored when the page is reloaded. All is pointing towards a [Single-page application](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-page_application).

At the same time, there is a community of opinionated web experts  that argue that we have become too dependent on JavaScript. We need to load a heavy framework just to deliver some static content, while HTML should work perfectly fine for this. On top of that JavaScript can be used in all kinds of malicious ways to spy on users, inject malicious code, etc. There is a [small group of users](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45824962) that navigates the web with JavaScript entirely disabled, which can be done from the settings of most browsers. Should you cater to them? Realistically speaking, unless you are offering a public service, probably not. But I like a challenge.

#### State in the URL
How do we know which windows to show users when we answer their requests from the server? The answer is quite simple, and it goes back to the first principle of the web: [hyperlinks](https://info.cern.ch/hypertext/WWW/WhatIs.html). The "H" in HTML stands for HyperText. We can use links to link to different pages on the site. Even though our UI might be dynamic, stateful, and use client-side routing, we can still use this principle to organize our UI. Every button triggering a window to open, is also a link. By default, this triggers a new page to be fetched from the server, which works for any user. If the JavaScript loads, this navigation is cancelled, and instead we only open a new window in the existing view. This way, the user with JavaScript never leaves the page and gets the smoothes experience, while the user without JavaScript can still read most of the content including the blog.

#### Interactivity

Of course not all the interactive features would work without JavaScript. The [paint widget](/paint) is entirely disabled. But we can still do a lot with modern web features. Windows can still be resized with the [resize feature](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/Properties/resize). We can have a custom menu using the [Popover API](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Global_attributes/popover), which can be used imperatively. By focusing on no-JS solutions, you might discover that you can replace your code with HTML-only solutions, reducing your JavaScript bundle and often making it easier to be accessible. Speaking of which...


### Accessibility

I wanted to make sure that the website was not only usable but also accessible. This could probably be its own post, so I will keep it short for now. The usual suspects were tackled: sufficient contrast between foreground and background, proper labeling where applicable, and controls to disable animations. Specific effort needed to be done to make the task bar and menus inside the [paint widget](/paint) usable for keyboard navigation. For this, the [toolbar pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/toolbar/) was implemented, including a [roving tabindex](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/practices/keyboard-interface/#kbd_roving_tabindex). I'm sure there is more to say about this part, and more to improve! It's a fine line between creativity and usability, and that's something I'm essentially exploring in this project. I know of a lot of developer blogs that keep a very minimal UI, just the basics. That would make my job a lot easier 😜


### What to expect

This will be the new home for my personal projects and ideas. Most prominently I want to start building my blog, writing about (mainly) technical stuff. Recurring topics will be accessibility, design systems, and general commentary on things happening in web development. And from time to time, more personal stories in relation to my other interests (fitness, food/cooking, travel, life in The Netherlands, etc.).

As for the code of this project, once I'm really happy with the OS, I will probably open-source it as a template, allowing others to use it for their own personal sites. For now, I'm publishing as-is, since there is always more to do and I want to start writing blog posts.
